Rosjanie, Ukraińcy i Białorusini: Jeden Naród Czy Wielkie Kłamstwo Historii?

Tekst materiału

Are Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians really three different peoples?

Or are they just three branches of one old East Slavic family, separated by borders, politics, and modern propaganda?

This question sounds simple. But the answer is not simple at all.

Because if you ask it in Moscow, Kyiv, Minsk, Warsaw, or New York, you may get five completely different answers. Some people will say: “Of course they are the same. Same roots, same religion, similar language, shared history.” Others will say: “No. They are absolutely different nations, with different memories, different political traditions, different cultures, and different historical experiences.”

And both answers can be partly true — and partly misleading.

Here is the real answer: Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians are closely related East Slavic peoples. They share deep historical roots, linguistic similarities, religious traditions, and centuries of contact. But they are not the same people. They became three different modern nations because their histories moved in different directions.

Think of it like three rivers that begin in the same ancient landscape. At first, they flow close together. Sometimes they even merge. But over time, they pass through different lands, different empires, different wars, different churches, different languages, and different ideas of freedom and power. By the time they reach the modern world, they are connected — but they are not identical.

In this video, we will look at ten major differences between Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians. Not to rank them. Not to say one is older, better, purer, or more “real.” That kind of thinking is dangerous and historically wrong.

The goal is different: to understand how three related peoples became three distinct nations.

We will look at language, history, religion, geography, political culture, national myths, food, folklore, identity, and memory. And by the end, you may see why the phrase “one people” is too simple — but the phrase “completely unrelated peoples” is also false.

Let’s begin.

Difference number one: the same root, but different historical branches.

Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians all belong to the East Slavic world. Their early medieval history is connected with the lands of Kyivan Rus, a powerful medieval state centered around Kyiv. That is why all three peoples can claim some connection to that early Rus heritage.

But here is where the misunderstanding begins.

Having a shared medieval background does not mean modern nations are the same. France, Italy, Spain, Portugal, and Romania all have deep connections to the Roman world and Latin language. But nobody says they are one people today. The same logic applies here.

After the decline of Kyivan Rus and the Mongol invasions, different East Slavic lands moved into different political worlds.

The northeastern lands, including the region around Moscow, developed under the influence of the Mongol-Tatar world and later became the center of the Grand Duchy of Moscow. This eventually grew into the Russian Tsardom and then the Russian Empire.

Many lands of today’s Belarus and Ukraine moved into the orbit of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and later the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. That meant different laws, different elites, different religious pressures, and different cultural influences.

Parts of Ukraine also developed a powerful Cossack tradition on the steppe frontier. That frontier experience shaped Ukrainian identity in a way that is very different from Moscow’s centralized state tradition.

So the first difference is this: all three peoples share early East Slavic roots, but they did not grow from one uninterrupted historical line. They grew through different states, different empires, and different political experiences.

Russia’s historical imagination often looks toward Moscow, the tsars, empire, Orthodoxy, and great-power statehood.

Ukraine’s historical imagination often looks toward Kyiv, the Cossacks, the steppe frontier, struggles for autonomy, and later the fight for independence.

Belarus’s historical imagination is more complicated: it often looks toward Polotsk, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, Soviet experience, and a quieter, less state-centered national tradition.

That is the first key point: common roots do not erase different historical development.

Difference number two: language — similar, but not the same.

Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian are all East Slavic languages. That means they are related. A person who knows one of them may recognize many words in the others. The grammar can feel familiar. The alphabet is Cyrillic in all three cases, although each language uses it differently.

But similar does not mean identical.

Russian is the most globally widespread of the three. It became the language of the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union, and still functions as a major language across the post-Soviet world.

Ukrainian is the state language of Ukraine. It has its own vocabulary, pronunciation, grammar patterns, literature, and emotional world. It is not “Russian with an accent.” In many ways, Ukrainian sounds closer to Belarusian than to Russian, and it also has historical contact with Polish and other Central European languages.

Belarusian is also an East Slavic language, but its modern situation is unique. Belarus has two official languages: Belarusian and Russian. In daily life, Russian has often dominated public space, cities, media, and education, while Belarusian has remained deeply important as a marker of national culture, literature, rural heritage, and political identity.

There are also mixed forms: in Ukraine, you may hear Surzhyk, a mixture of Ukrainian and Russian elements. In Belarus, you may hear Trasianka, a mixture of Belarusian and Russian elements. These mixtures are not just “bad language.” They are signs of centuries of contact, pressure, bilingualism, and social change.

Language is one of the strongest markers of difference, but also one of the most misunderstood.

A Russian speaker in Ukraine is not automatically Russian.

A Belarusian who speaks Russian every day is not automatically Russian.

A Ukrainian who grew up bilingual is not less Ukrainian.

In this region, language and identity overlap — but they are not the same thing.

The important point is this: Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian are sister languages. But they are separate languages with separate literary traditions, separate national meanings, and separate emotional associations.

Difference number three: geography shaped different mental worlds.

Geography is not destiny, but it matters.

Russia is enormous. It stretches from Eastern Europe across Siberia to the Pacific Ocean. Russian identity developed inside a vast continental state, with forests, rivers, steppe, tundra, mountains, and borders with Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia, China, and the Pacific world.

That scale shaped Russian political imagination. Russia often thinks in terms of space, security, frontiers, empire, and strategic depth. The Russian state historically expanded across massive territories. This created a culture where the state, the army, borders, and central authority became extremely important.

Ukraine is different. Ukraine sits between Central Europe, the Black Sea, the steppe, and the old routes between north and south. Its geography includes fertile black-earth agricultural lands, the Dnipro River, the Black Sea coast, and the steppe frontier. For centuries, Ukraine was a zone of contact — and conflict — between Poland-Lithuania, Muscovy, the Ottoman world, the Crimean Tatars, Austria-Hungary, and local Cossack power.

This helped create a political culture connected to frontier life, agriculture, local autonomy, and resistance to outside domination.

Belarus has a different geography again. It is a land of forests, rivers, lakes, marshes, and crossroads between Poland, Lithuania, Russia, and Ukraine. Belarusian lands often stood in the middle of larger powers. This created a historical experience of borderlands, survival, adaptation, and cultural blending.

That is why the mental maps are different.

Russia often sees itself as a huge civilization-state.

Ukraine often sees itself as a European borderland that became a nation through struggle.

Belarus often sees itself as a quieter crossroads country, with a strong sense of place but a more complicated public national narrative.

Same region? Yes.

Same geography? No.

And geography helped shape different habits, economies, symbols, and fears.

Difference number four: different state traditions.

This may be one of the biggest differences.

Russian historical statehood developed around Moscow. Over time, Moscow became a centralized power. The ruler, the church, the army, and the bureaucracy were closely connected. The Russian Tsardom, and later the Russian Empire, created a powerful tradition of vertical authority.

That does not mean every Russian person loves authoritarianism. That would be unfair and false. But historically, the Russian state tradition has been strongly centralized.

Ukraine’s political tradition is more fragmented and more contested. Parts of Ukraine were under Lithuania, Poland, Austria-Hungary, the Russian Empire, the Ottoman sphere, and other powers. But one of the most powerful symbols of Ukrainian historical identity is the Cossack Hetmanate — a military-political order associated with elected leaders, frontier freedom, and local self-organization.

Again, we should not romanticize it too much. Cossack society had hierarchy, violence, and its own inequalities. But as a national symbol, the Cossack represents something very different from the image of the tsar. He represents freedom, resistance, and self-rule.

Belarusian state tradition is more subtle. Belarusian lands were important inside the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. But modern Belarusian national statehood appeared later and with many interruptions. Belarusian identity often developed through language, folklore, local memory, and culture more than through a long continuous independent state.

This creates three different relationships with the state.

Russian identity has often been tied to a strong state.

Ukrainian identity has often been tied to the struggle for a state.

Belarusian identity has often been tied to the search for a state and the preservation of culture under stronger neighbors.

That is a major difference.

Difference number five: religion — shared Orthodoxy, different religious landscapes.

All three peoples have deep ties to Eastern Orthodox Christianity. The Christianization of Kyivan Rus in the tenth century is a major event in the shared history of the region.

But religion did not develop in exactly the same way everywhere.

In Russia, the Russian Orthodox Church became closely connected to the Russian state. Moscow developed the idea of itself as a major Orthodox center. Over time, Orthodoxy became one of the pillars of Russian imperial identity, even though Russia also includes Muslims, Buddhists, Jews, Catholics, Protestants, and many other communities.

In Ukraine, Orthodoxy is also central. But Ukraine’s religious map is more plural. There is the Orthodox tradition, but also the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, especially important in western Ukraine. There are Roman Catholics, Protestants, Jews, Muslims, especially Crimean Tatars, and other groups. Ukraine’s church history is also tied to the tension between Moscow-linked religious institutions and Ukrainian church independence.

In Belarus, Orthodoxy is also important, but there is a visible Catholic presence, especially in western regions. Belarusian lands historically stood between Orthodox and Catholic worlds. That created a religious landscape shaped by both Eastern and Western Christianity.

So again, it is not “same religion, same people.”

Religion connects them — but the religious institutions, political meanings, and regional patterns differ.

In Russia, Orthodoxy has often been linked to imperial continuity.

In Ukraine, religion has become connected to national independence, especially in recent decades.

In Belarus, religion reflects a borderland between Orthodox and Catholic influences, often mixed with a strong Soviet secular inheritance.

Difference number six: different national heroes and cultural symbols.

If you want to understand a nation, ask: whose portraits are on the walls? Whose poems do children learn? Whose statues stand in the main square? Whose suffering is remembered? Whose victories are celebrated?

For Russians, major cultural symbols often include figures like Alexander Pushkin, Leo Tolstoy, Fyodor Dostoevsky, Peter the Great, Catherine the Great, Yuri Gagarin, and victory in World War II. Russian culture also has a massive literary and artistic tradition that became global: novels, ballet, classical music, theater, film, and philosophy.

For Ukrainians, one of the central figures is Taras Shevchenko — poet, artist, and national symbol. He represents not just literature, but the voice of the people, the pain of oppression, and the dream of freedom. Other key symbols include the Cossacks, the trident symbol, the blue and yellow flag, the Dnipro River, the Maidan, and the memory of struggles for independence.

For Belarusians, major cultural figures include Yanka Kupala, Yakub Kolas, Francysk Skaryna, and Euphrosyne of Polotsk. Belarusian cultural identity is often tied to poetry, language revival, printing traditions, folklore, and the memory of the land itself — forests, villages, songs, and quiet endurance.

The emotional tone is different.

Russian culture often presents itself on an imperial and universal scale: big novels, big states, big questions about humanity, suffering, God, power, and destiny.

Ukrainian culture often centers on freedom, land, song, loss, resistance, and dignity.

Belarusian culture often carries themes of memory, modesty, survival, language preservation, and life between larger forces.

Of course, these are broad patterns, not strict rules. Every culture is diverse. But national symbols matter because they teach people what kind of story they belong to.

Difference number seven: folklore, food, and everyday culture.

At first glance, everyday culture may look similar. There are villages, Orthodox holidays, folk songs, embroidery, soups, dumplings, pancakes, rye bread, potatoes, mushrooms, berries, pickles, and family feasts across the region.

But when you look closer, the details differ.

Ukrainian folk culture is famous for its embroidered shirts, or vyshyvankas, with strong regional patterns. Ukrainian music often has a bright, lyrical, sometimes dramatic sound. Ukrainian cuisine is strongly associated with borscht, varenyky, salo, holubtsi, pampushky, and dishes tied to the agricultural richness of the land.

Belarusian cuisine is often associated with potatoes — especially draniki, potato pancakes — as well as machanka, rye bread, mushrooms, forest foods, dairy dishes, and simple hearty village cooking. Belarusian folk culture has a softer, more forest-and-village feeling, with songs and traditions shaped by the landscape of wetlands, woods, and small settlements.

Russian cuisine is extremely broad because Russia itself is enormous and multiethnic. People may think of blini, shchi, pelmeni, kasha, pirozhki, kvass, pickles, and tea culture. But Russian food also absorbed influences from Siberia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, the Volga region, and many non-Russian peoples inside the Russian Federation.

Folk clothing also differs. Embroidery patterns, colors, headwear, and wedding traditions vary by region. A Ukrainian vyshyvanka is not the same as a Belarusian embroidered shirt, and neither is identical to Russian folk dress.

The same applies to music and dance. Outsiders may hear “Slavic folk song” and put everything in one box. But people from the region often recognize differences in melody, rhythm, pronunciation, and emotional style.

Everyday culture is where difference becomes visible in small details: what people cook for holidays, which songs make grandparents cry, how weddings are celebrated, what words are used at home, what jokes people tell, and what childhood memories feel familiar.

Difference number eight: historical trauma and collective memory.

This is one of the most sensitive differences.

Nations are shaped not only by what they celebrate, but also by what they mourn.

In Russia, one of the central memories is the Great Patriotic War — the Soviet fight against Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1945. The suffering was enormous. The victory became one of the strongest pillars of modern Russian identity. For many Russians, World War II is not just history. It is sacred family memory.

Belarus also suffered catastrophically during World War II. A huge portion of the population was killed, villages were destroyed, and the partisan struggle became central to Belarusian Soviet and post-Soviet memory. Belarusian official identity has often emphasized wartime suffering, resistance, and liberation.

Ukraine also suffered terribly during World War II, but Ukrainian memory is more contested because the land was caught between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, and because different regions had different experiences. In addition, Ukraine has another central trauma: the Holodomor, the man-made famine of 1932–1933, which many Ukrainians understand as a national catastrophe and an attack on the Ukrainian people.

This creates different emotional histories.

Russia often frames the twentieth century through victory, sacrifice, industrial power, and the defeat of fascism.

Belarus often frames it through occupation, destruction, partisan resistance, and survival.

Ukraine often frames it through multiple traumas: famine, repression, war, occupation, deportations, Soviet control, and repeated struggles for sovereignty.

Again, this does not mean ordinary people all think the same way. Families are complicated. Memories differ by region, generation, and politics. But national memory matters.

When three peoples remember the same century differently, they become different political communities.

Difference number nine: relationship to empire and independence.

This is another major difference.

Russia was the center of an empire. The Russian Empire and later the Soviet Union were ruled from Moscow. Even though many Russians also suffered under imperial and Soviet systems, Russian national identity has often been connected to state expansion, great-power status, and the idea of a special civilizational mission.

Ukraine was usually not the center of empire. It was more often a land divided between empires or ruled by larger powers. That is why independence has such a powerful meaning in Ukrainian identity. Ukrainian history is often told as a long struggle to preserve language, land, culture, and political self-determination.

Belarus was also usually not the center of empire. It was ruled by or included in larger political systems: the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Russian Empire, the Soviet Union. Modern Belarusian independence came in 1991, but the country’s post-Soviet identity remained deeply connected to Soviet symbols, Russian language, and political dependence on Moscow.

So the relationship to empire is different.

For Russia, empire is often remembered as greatness, security, and historical mission — though many Russians criticize this view.

For Ukraine, empire is often remembered as domination, Russification, and the denial of Ukrainian identity.

For Belarus, empire is remembered more ambiguously: it includes loss, Soviet nostalgia, cultural suppression, modernization, and dependence.

This difference explains why political language can sound completely different.

When a Russian speaker says “historical unity,” a Ukrainian may hear “loss of sovereignty.”

When a Russian speaker says “brotherly peoples,” a Belarusian opposition activist may hear “assimilation.”

When a Ukrainian says “independence,” some Russians may hear “anti-Russian politics,” even though for Ukrainians it often means basic national survival.

The same words carry different memories.

Difference number ten: modern identity — civic nation, ethnic nation, imperial nation, borderland nation.

Modern identity may be the most important difference today.

Russia is a multiethnic federation. Ethnic Russians are the majority, but Russia also includes Tatars, Bashkirs, Chechens, Chuvash, Avars, Yakuts, Buryats, and many other peoples. So Russian identity has two layers: ethnic Russian identity and state-based Russian citizenship. In English, this is confusing because both are often translated as “Russian.” But in Russian, there is a difference between “russkiy,” ethnic or cultural Russian, and “rossiyskiy,” citizen of the Russian state.

Ukraine has increasingly developed a civic national identity. That means a Ukrainian can be Ukrainian-speaking or Russian-speaking, Orthodox, Catholic, Jewish, Muslim, atheist, Crimean Tatar, Jewish Ukrainian, Armenian Ukrainian, or ethnic Russian Ukrainian — and still be part of the Ukrainian political nation. Since 2014, and especially since 2022, Ukrainian identity has become more strongly tied to sovereignty, democracy, resistance, and the idea that citizenship matters more than ethnic origin.

Belarusian identity is in a different situation. Many Belarusians speak Russian in daily life, but still feel Belarusian. The Belarusian language has become a powerful cultural and political symbol for many people, even if not everyone uses it every day. The white-red-white flag, the Pahonia symbol, Belarusian poetry, and the memory of protests have become meaningful symbols for parts of society, while the official state promotes a more Soviet-style and Russia-oriented identity.

So the modern question is not just: are they ethnically different?

The better question is: what kind of nation are they imagining?

Russia often imagines itself as a great power and civilization-state.

Ukraine increasingly imagines itself as a civic nation defending its sovereignty.

Belarus is caught between official state identity, Russian influence, Soviet memory, and a revived national-cultural identity.

This is why the question “Are they different peoples?” cannot be answered only with DNA, language, or medieval history.

A nation is not just blood. It is memory, institutions, language, culture, choices, and shared political experience.

Now let’s address the most dangerous myth: the idea of pure peoples.

There are no pure peoples here.

Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians have mixed with each other for centuries. Families cross borders. Millions of people have relatives in more than one country. There are Ukrainian surnames in Russia, Russian speakers in Ukraine, Belarusian families in Poland and Lithuania, Polish heritage in Belarus and Ukraine, Jewish history across the region, Crimean Tatar history in Ukraine, Turkic and Finno-Ugric influences in Russia, Baltic influences in Belarus, and many more layers.

So if someone tries to reduce this topic to blood, race, or “real ethnicity,” they are already wrong.

The differences are real — but they are historical, cultural, linguistic, political, and social. They are not about one people being cleaner, older, or superior.

The three peoples are related. But related peoples can become different nations.

A brother is not the same person as his brother.

A cousin is not the same person as his cousin.

A shared grandmother does not mean one shared identity forever.

So, are Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians different peoples?

Yes — in the modern national sense, they are different peoples.

Are they completely unrelated?

No. They are closely related East Slavic peoples with shared roots and centuries of cultural contact.

The real answer is somewhere between two false extremes.

The first false extreme says: “They are all one people, and their separate identities are artificial.” That is wrong because Ukrainian and Belarusian identities have real historical, linguistic, and cultural depth.

The second false extreme says: “They have nothing in common.” That is also wrong because the shared East Slavic foundation, Orthodox heritage, family ties, and cultural overlap are undeniable.

The truth is more interesting.

They are connected — but distinct.

They are similar — but not interchangeable.

They share roots — but not the same historical destiny.

And the more you study them, the more you realize that the question is not simply about the past. It is also about power.

Who gets to define identity?

Can a large nation decide that a smaller neighbor is not real?

Can language survive if the state pushes another language into schools, cities, and media?

Can people choose a civic identity even if their family language is different?

Can history be shared without being used as a weapon?

These are not academic questions. They shape real lives.

A Ukrainian who speaks Russian may still fight for Ukraine.

A Belarusian who grew up speaking Russian may still dream in Belarusian symbols.

A Russian who rejects empire may feel close to Ukrainian and Belarusian culture while still being Russian.

Identity is not a prison. It is a living story.

But that story must be chosen, not imposed.

So when someone says, “Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians are one people,” ask one more question: who benefits from saying that?

And when someone says, “They are completely different and have nothing in common,” ask the same question: what history is being erased?

The strongest answer is the most honest one:

Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians are three related East Slavic peoples who developed into three distinct modern nations through different languages, different state traditions, different religious landscapes, different memories, and different political choices.

Their histories touch.

Their cultures overlap.

Their families are often connected.

But their national identities are not the same.

And understanding that difference is not about division.

It is about respect.

Because real respect begins when you stop forcing people into the identity that is convenient for someone else — and start listening to how they define themselves.

That is the difference between shared history and ownership.

Shared history says: we have roots in common.

Ownership says: because we have roots in common, you belong to me.

And that is where history becomes dangerous.

So the final answer is simple, but not simplistic:

Yes, Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians are different peoples.

Not because they have no connection.

But because connection is not the same as sameness.

And in Eastern Europe, that difference has shaped centuries of history — and it still shapes the world today.
